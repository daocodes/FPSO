## Abstract

Constrained portfolio selection under cardinality and bounding constraints is a mixed-integer problem for which exact solvers scale poorly and gradient methods stall in local optima. Population-based metaheuristics fill this gap, but each carries a documented deficiency: Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) loses swarm diversity and converges prematurely, while the Firefly Algorithm (FA) maintains diversity but lacks precision near the optimum. We propose FPSO, a hybrid whose update rule blends firefly attraction toward a brightness-weighted centroid of superior peers with PSO refinement toward personal and global bests, under a repair operator that keeps the search inside the feasible set. We then use FPSO as the vehicle for a controlled study of a claim that is widely made and rarely tested: that conditioning a portfolio optimizer on a detected market regime improves risk-adjusted performance.

We evaluate 20 experimental arms over CRSP daily data from 2011 to 2024, with 30 matched seeds per arm and four transaction-cost levels, injecting the regime signal at two distinct points — into the optimizer's *preferences* (its hyperparameters) and into its *beliefs* (its covariance estimate). Both mechanisms appear to work: net of 1.5% costs they gain +0.113 and +0.388 Sharpe over a static baseline. Both effects vanish under control. A permanently-calm parameterization with no timing at all beats the regime-switched one; a flat five-year covariance window with no regime model beats regime-weighted covariance driven by perfect future-knowledge labels; and across 17 comparable arms, net-of-cost performance is ordered almost perfectly by realized turnover alone (Spearman rho = −0.94, p = 1.8 × 10⁻⁸), while the same correlation computed gross of costs is insignificant.

We settle the question with a turnover-matched control: a static arm whose turnover penalty is solved by bisection until it trades exactly as much as the regime arm. Against this control the regime effect is −0.062 and −0.043 Sharpe — negative at every cost level — and the cost-conditionality that made regime conditioning look valuable collapses by 95% and 97%. We conclude that in this setting the measured regime effect is entirely the turnover the regime mechanism happens to induce. Our contribution is the evaluation protocol that demonstrates this, which caught two silent confounds in our own study before they reached publication, and which we release alongside the FPSO implementation.

# 1. Introduction {#sec:intro}

Modern Portfolio Theory, canonically formulated by @Markowitz1952, frames portfolio construction as a mean-variance optimization problem that seeks to minimize portfolio variance for a target level of expected return. Despite the usefulness of this mean-variance (MV) framework, practical investment settings often require additional constraints. Investors may impose limits on the number of assets held (cardinality constraints), minimum and maximum portfolio weights (bounding constraints), transaction lot requirements, sector exposure limits, and turnover budgets. These constraints transform the original convex optimization problem into a mixed-integer optimization problem that is much more complex in nature.

Classical exact solvers such as branch-and-bound and cutting-plane methods scale poorly beyond a few hundred assets. Similarly, gradient-based local search is fatally susceptible to the many local optima generated by cardinality penalties. As a result, biologically-inspired metaheuristics — including Genetic Algorithms (GA) [@Holland1975], Simulated Annealing (SA) [@Kirkpatrick1983], Tabu Search (TS) [@Glover1989], and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) [@Kennedy1995] — have been extensively applied to constrained portfolio selection, offering population-based global search with no gradient requirement while being able to explore large search spaces.

However, each method carries well-documented deficiencies: GA suffers from premature convergence and parameter sensitivity; SA is computationally slow for large populations; TS requires problem-specific neighborhood design; and PSO is prone to loss of swarm diversity and subsequent entrapment in local optima [@Engelbrecht2007].

More recently, the portfolio optimization problem has expanded to include artificial intelligence methods that depart from traditional search heuristics. Among these, Decision-Focused Learning (DFL) has emerged as a powerful architecture that deviates from standard two-stage "predict-then-optimize" pipelines. Instead, DFL embeds the optimization layer directly into the neural network's training step, mapping asset features straight to portfolio weights. At the same time, Large Language Models (LLMs) are being explored as zero-shot portfolio allocators, feeding historical prices, macro signals, and fundamental metrics into generative models to build portfolios. While these AI approaches offer strong predictive advantages, explicitly managing complex, non-convex constraints remains a core challenge. This is precisely the area where constraint-aware metaheuristics retain an advantage: they handle hard combinatorial constraints natively, without requiring a gradient.

The Firefly Algorithm (FA), introduced by @Yang2008 [@Yang2009], is a population-based optimization method in which each firefly represents a candidate solution. The quality of a solution is measured by its brightness, with better solutions assigned higher brightness values. During the search process, fireflies move toward brighter fireflies, and the strength of attraction decreases with distance. This mechanism inherently partitions the swarm into local attraction basins and helps maintain population diversity. However, while FA is effective at exploring new solutions, it can have less precision near the global optimum. This complementary weakness is precisely the inverse of PSO's documented limitation.

The identification of complementary weaknesses between PSO and FA provides clear motivation for hybridization: PSO offers refinement capability but lacks diversity; FA offers diversity but lacks precision. Recent hybrids combining PSO with genetic algorithms and collaborative neurodynamic optimization frameworks incorporating PSO [@Leung2022] have demonstrated that strategic integration of complementary metaheuristics produces superior performance in constrained portfolio problems. A structured balance between exploration and exploitation has been shown to be essential for solving NP-hard portfolio optimization with cardinality constraints [@WoodsideOriakhi2011]. Despite these successes, the balance of exploration and exploitation remains unresolved for a specific region: the cardinality and bounding constraints generate a multimodal landscape where PSO loses diversity too quickly to find optimal solutions, while FA spreads out too much to achieve high precision. Neither method solves both issues on its own.

Our work sits at the intersection of three research streams: metaheuristic approaches to constrained portfolio selection, the Firefly Algorithm and its hybrids, and the recent wave of learning-based portfolio construction. We review each in turn and identify the gap FPSO addresses.

## 3.1 Metaheuristics for Constrained Portfolio Selection

Augmenting the mean-variance model of @Markowitz1952 with cardinality and bounding constraints transforms it into a mixed-integer quadratic program for which exact solution scales poorly. @Chang2000 provided the seminal metaheuristic treatment, adapting GA, TS, and SA to trace the cardinality-constrained efficient frontier and establishing the public benchmark instances used by much of the subsequent literature. @WoodsideOriakhi2011 revisited these instances with improved population heuristics and demonstrated that combining complementary search operators yielded better results than any single heuristic alone. A broad survey by @Ertenlice2018 documents that swarm intelligence has since become the dominant metaheuristic family for portfolio optimization, with PSO the most frequently applied algorithm.

Within the PSO line, @Cura2009 first applied standalone PSO to the cardinality-constrained problem, finding it competitive with GA, TS, and SA, and strongest on low-risk portfolios. @Zhuy2011 reported that PSO outperformed GA on Sharpe ratio across both constrained and unconstrained formulations. However, @Deng2012 showed that standard PSO stagnates on larger cardinality-constrained instances as swarm diversity collapses, and proposed reflection-based modifications to compensate. This premature-convergence failure mode is well documented in the broader literature [@Engelbrecht2007] and is the specific weakness our hybridization targets.

## 3.2 The Firefly Algorithm and Hybrid Metaheuristics

The Firefly Algorithm [@Yang2008; @Yang2009] replaces attraction to a single global best with pairwise attraction that decays exponentially with distance, causing the swarm to self-organize into multiple local attraction basins. This makes FA naturally suited to the multimodal landscapes that cardinality penalties induce, and a substantial literature of FA variants and applications has developed [@Fister2013]. In finance, @Bacanin2014 applied FA to cardinality-constrained mean-variance portfolio selection with an entropy diversity constraint, reporting results superior to GA, TS, SA, and PSO baselines on standard benchmarks. The complementary weakness of FA is also well documented: its continuous random movements make it slow to hone in on precise, optimal solutions [@Fister2013].

Hybrids that pair FA's exploration with PSO's exploitation have shown promise outside of finance. @Aydilek2018 proposed HFPSO (Hybrid Firefly Particle Swarm Optimization), which interleaves firefly-style local search within a PSO loop and outperformed both standalone algorithms on computationally expensive numerical benchmarks. Separately, weighted-centroid aggregation rules — in which an agent moves toward an attractiveness-weighted centroid of superior peers rather than a single leader — have proven effective in other swarm-based estimation settings such as wireless sensor network localization [@Yu2025]; FPSO's multi-peer centroid update (Section 5.2) adapts this mechanism to portfolio search.

In the portfolio domain itself, the closest work is the collaborative neurodynamic framework of @Leung2022, which couples multiple projection neural networks through a PSO-style information exchange to solve cardinality-constrained portfolio selection. That approach, however, relies on solving convex subproblems with gradient information at each step, whereas FPSO uses no gradients and handles constraints through a repair-dominant scheme, making it agnostic to the smoothness of the objective.

## 3.3 Learning-Based Portfolio Construction

A parallel literature replaces search with learning. Decision-Focused Learning trains the predictive model through the downstream optimization layer so that forecast error is measured in decision quality rather than statistical accuracy [@Elmachtoub2022]. Recent ICAIF work has applied DFL to portfolio construction directly: @Kim2025 learn covariance estimates end-to-end for the global minimum variance portfolio, and @Lee2025 characterize how DFL reshapes return forecasting models for mean-variance selection. In a different direction, @Hou2024 evaluate LLMs as zero-shot allocators that map prices, macro signals, and fundamentals to weights via structured prompts. While these approaches offer strong predictive power, embedding hard combinatorial constraints into differentiable layers or generative prompts remains largely unresolved, and simple heuristics remain difficult to beat: @DeMiguel2009 famously showed that naive 1/N diversification survives comparison with far more sophisticated estimators.

A long line of work models markets as switching between latent states and conditions allocation on the inferred state. Hidden Markov and Markov-switching models are the dominant formulation, and reported results are generally favorable: regime-aware allocation is found to improve risk-adjusted performance, particularly through reduced drawdowns in crisis states.

Two features of this literature motivate our study. First, the regime signal is injected at different points by different authors — sometimes into the risk model, sometimes into risk aversion or the allocation rule — and these are rarely distinguished or compared within a single design. Second, and more importantly, regime-conditioned strategies almost always trade less than their unconditional counterparts, because a persistent state estimate produces persistent allocations. When performance is reported net of transaction costs, turnover and regime conditioning are therefore confounded by construction, and we are not aware of published work that separates them with a control matched on turnover. Section 8 shows that in our setting this confound accounts for the entire effect.

## 3.5 Positioning of This Work

In summary: metaheuristics handle hard portfolio constraints natively, but each standalone algorithm carries a documented exploration or exploitation deficiency; FA–PSO hybrids that remedy exactly this tradeoff exist in other domains but have not been applied to cardinality-constrained portfolio selection with a multi-peer centroid attraction rule under repair-based constraint handling; and learning-based allocators sidestep search entirely at the cost of constraint fidelity. Orthogonally, the regime-switching literature reports effects that have not been separated from the turnover those mechanisms induce. This paper addresses both gaps: FPSO fills the first, and the controlled design of Sections 6–8 fills the second.

# 5. The FPSO Algorithm {#sec:fpso}

## 5.1 Mathematical Basis

The basis of any portfolio optimization problem is the decision of which linear combination of stocks and weights $w_i$ maximizes the portfolio return $R(w)$ subject to $\sum_i w_i = 1$. We express this relation as

$$
R(\mathbf{w}) = \boldsymbol{\mu}^\top \mathbf{w} - \lambda_v \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}} - \lambda_t \cdot \mathrm{TO}(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{prev}})
\tag{1}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}^\top \mathbf{w}$ is the expected portfolio return, $\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}}$ is the portfolio standard deviation, $\lambda_v$ is the risk-aversion weight,

$$
\mathrm{TO}(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{prev}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{prev}} \rVert_1
\tag{2}
$$

is one-way turnover, and $\lambda_t$ is the turnover penalty. The remaining constraints are cardinality — the number of assets with $w_i > 0$ is bounded by $K$ — and a box constraint $l_i \leq w_i \leq u_i$ on each position.

Our fitness function augments $R$ with penalties for constraint violation:

$$
\Phi(\mathbf{w}) = R(\mathbf{w})
- \rho_1 \left| \sum_{i=1}^N w_i - 1 \right|
- \rho_2 \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \max(0, l_i - w_i)^2 + \max(0, w_i - u_i)^2 \right]
- \rho_3 \left( \mathrm{card}(\mathbf{w}) - K \right)_+^2
\tag{3}
$$

where $\rho_1$ is the budget penalty, $\rho_2$ the box penalty, and $\rho_3$ the cardinality penalty.

## 5.2 Swarm Mechanics

Brightness (or luminance) is defined for each particle $i$ at time $t$ as

$$
I_i(t) = \Phi(\mathbf{x}_i(t)) - \Phi_{\min}(t) + \epsilon
\tag{4}
$$

where $\Phi_{\min}(t)$ is the worst fitness in the swarm at time $t$ and $\epsilon$ is a small positive constant. The subtraction guarantees non-negative brightness, and $\epsilon$ keeps the worst particle from having exactly zero attractive power.

Once brightness is known for all particles, we compute how each particle moves toward its brighter peers. In traditional PSO all particles move toward the single brightest particle in the swarm; in FPSO each particle moves toward the attractiveness-weighted centroid of *all* brighter particles. We first identify the brighter peers of particle $i$:

$$
\mathcal{B}_i(t) = \{\, j : I_j(t) > I_i(t) \,\}
\tag{5}
$$

The centroid is then

$$
\mathbf{a}_i(t) = \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{B}_i(t)} \beta_0 e^{-\gamma r_{ij}^2} I_j(t)\, \mathbf{x}_j(t)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{B}_i(t)} \beta_0 e^{-\gamma r_{ij}^2} I_j(t) + \delta}
\tag{6}
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_i(t)$ is the position of firefly $i$ at time $t$, $r_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf{x}_i(t) - \mathbf{x}_j(t) \rVert_2$ is the Euclidean distance between fireflies $i$ and $j$, $\beta_0$ is the attractiveness at zero distance, $\gamma$ is the light absorption coefficient that dampens attraction with distance, and $\delta$ is a small constant preventing division by zero when $\mathcal{B}_i(t)$ is empty.

\tag{8}
$$

with inertia $\omega$, cognitive and social coefficients $c_1, c_2$, and $u_1, u_2 \sim U(0,1)$. Section 5.3 gives the rule that combines Eq. 7 and Eq. 8 into a single move.

```
Algorithm 1: FPSO
Input:  expected returns mu, covariance Sigma, previous weights w_prev,
        swarm size n, iteration budget T, blend coefficient phi
Output: optimized portfolio weights g

 1  Initialize swarm S = {x_1, ..., x_n} of random weight vectors
 2  for each x_i in S:  x_i <- Repair(x_i)          // simplex, box, cardinality
 3  Evaluate Phi(x_i) for all i; set p_i <- x_i;  v_i <- 0
 4  g <- argmax_i Phi(p_i)
 5  for t = 1 to T:
 6      Compute brightness I_i for all i                            (Eq. 4)
 7      for each particle i:
 8          B_i  <- {j : I_j > I_i}                                 (Eq. 5)
 9          a_i  <- WeightedCentroid(B_i)                           (Eq. 6)
10          d_FA <- beta(r_i) * (a_i - x_i) + alpha * eps_i         (Eq. 7)
11          d_PSO<- omega*v_i + c1*u1*(p_i - x_i) + c2*u2*(g - x_i) (Eq. 8)
12          x_new <- Repair(x_i + (1 - phi) * d_FA + phi * d_PSO)   (Eq. 9)
13          v_i <- x_new - x_i        // realized displacement after projection
14          x_i <- x_new
15          if Phi(x_i) > Phi(p_i):  p_i <- x_i
16      g <- argmax_i Phi(p_i)
17  return g
```

Both update rules are evaluated on every iteration and combined by a **constant** blend coefficient $\phi$ (`pso_blend`):

$$
\mathbf{x}_i(t+1) = \mathrm{Repair}\!\left( \mathbf{x}_i(t) + (1-\phi)\, \mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{FA}}_i + \phi\, \mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{PSO}}_i \right)
\tag{9}
$$

with $\phi = 0.5$ throughout this study and $\phi = 0$ recovering pure FA (the ablation of Section 8.4). Note that $\mathbf{v}_i$ is set to the *realized* displacement after projection (line 13) rather than the proposed one, so PSO momentum never accumulates movement the constraints rejected.

The repair operator on lines 2 and 12 projects a candidate onto the feasible set by (i) zeroing all but the $K$ largest weights, (ii) clipping to the box $[l_i, u_i]$, and (iii) redistributing residual mass across unsaturated positions until the budget constraint binds. Because repair runs on every candidate at every iteration, the search path never leaves the feasible region.

# 6. Experimental Design {#sec:design}

## 6.1 Data

The empirical data for this study is drawn from the Wharton Research Data Services Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) daily securities database. We observe daily equity metrics for US common shares identified by their permanent security identifiers (`PERMNO`). The panel spans January 1, 2005 through December 31, 2024; the evaluation window runs from January 1, 2011 through December 31, 2024, with the preceding years reserved for feature standardization and regime-model burn-in.

## 6.2 Backtest Protocol

Portfolios are rebalanced monthly, giving 168 decisions over the evaluation window. At each rebalance the optimizer receives $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ estimated from a trailing window and the previous period's realized holdings as $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{prev}}$, and returns target weights that are executed at the next available price. Portfolio accounting is performed with `vectorbt` using target-percent orders under cash sharing, so that positions, costs, and drift are tracked consistently across arms.

Every arm is run at four transaction-cost levels — 0, 50, 100, and 150 basis points per unit of turnover — and at 30 seeds. Seeds are matched across arms: seed $s$ drives the same rebalance dates and the same initialization stream in every arm, which makes all cross-arm comparisons paired and removes seed variance from the difference.

**Causality.** The harness carries a machine-checked lookahead proof: the panel is deliberately poisoned after each decision date and the backtest re-run, asserting that decisions are bit-identical. Any dependence on future data would change them. The regime detector uses *filtered* posteriors $P(s_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t)$ rather than smoothed posteriors $P(s_t \mid \mathcal{F}_T)$, which is the distinction most commonly lost in regime-switching backtests and which alone can manufacture the effect this paper is testing for.

## 6.3 Statistical Method

Because arms are seed-matched, we report *paired* differences. Confidence intervals come from a stationary bootstrap [@PolitisRomano1994] on the paired daily return difference series, which preserves autocorrelation and volatility clustering that an i.i.d. bootstrap would destroy. Sharpe-ratio differences are additionally tested with the HAC-robust @LedoitWolf2008 statistic, which corrects for the non-normality and serial dependence that invalidate a naive $t$-test on Sharpe ratios. Where a family of arms is compared against a common baseline we apply a Holm correction and report both raw and adjusted $p$-values.

## 6.4 Evaluation Metric

FPSO optimizes absolute return (Eq. 1), but we report the Sharpe ratio as the primary evaluation metric. These are deliberately different: the optimizer targets the objective a fund manager is compensated on, while the evaluation asks whether the resulting portfolio is attractive on the risk-adjusted basis the literature uses for comparison. We report annualized return, volatility, Sortino ratio, maximum drawdown, Calmar ratio, and turnover alongside Sharpe so that this choice can be audited.

## 6.5 The Experimental Matrix

The study comprises 20 arms. Beyond the benchmarks of Section 4, the regime arms fall into four families.

**Detectors.** A three-state Gaussian HMM (`regime_hmm`) is the primary detector, fitted on realized volatility, a volatility ratio, trailing return, and drawdown, refitted annually with a 36-month burn-in and a two-month minimum dwell. States are canonically ordered by mean realized volatility into CALM, TURBULENT, and CRISIS. We also test a Gaussian mixture without the Markov structure (`regime_gmm`) and a transparent rule using volatility terciles with nothing fitted (`regime_volq`).

**Signal-free controls.** `regime_shuffled` and `moments_shuffled` permute the treatment arm's *realized label episodes*, which preserves state marginals and dwell-time distributions exactly while destroying only the timing. Any performance difference between a treatment arm and its shuffled control is attributable to *when* regimes are detected, not to how often each state occurs or how long it lasts.

**Lookahead oracles.** `regime_oracle` and `moments_oracle` fit the detector on the full sample and reveal the resulting state labels to the policy. These arms are causally invalid by construction and are reported as upper bounds: they answer "what is the most this mechanism could deliver if the detector were perfect?"

**Pinned parameterizations.** `pinned_calm` and `pinned_crisis` hold the regime-conditioned parameter block fixed at one state's values for the entire backtest, with no timing whatsoever. These separate the effect of *having* a particular parameterization from the effect of *switching to it at the right moment*.

# 7. Where the Regime Signal Enters {#sec:mechanisms}

A regime signal can enter a portfolio optimizer at two structurally different points, and the distinction turns out to matter more than the choice of detector. We build and test both.

## 7.1 Mechanism 1 — Regime-Conditioned Preferences

The detected regime selects FPSO's hyperparameters: exploration rate $\alpha$, attraction decay $\gamma$, risk aversion $\lambda_v$, turnover penalty $\lambda_t$, cardinality bound $K$, and position cap $u$. In CRISIS the optimizer becomes more risk-averse, trades less, and holds fewer, smaller positions. This is the formulation most directly analogous to regime-dependent risk aversion in the switching literature.

**Result: null.** `regime_hmm` versus `static` is −0.003 Sharpe at 50 bps (95% CI [−0.080, +0.073], $p = 0.96$).

**Why it fails.** Half of these knobs ($\alpha$, $\gamma$) govern *how FPSO searches*, and FPSO already converges — tuning them changes the route, not the destination. The other half change the objective, but they act on $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ estimated from a flat 252-day window that is itself regime-blind. In March 2020 the mechanism tells the optimizer to fear risk while handing it a covariance matrix dominated by the preceding eleven calm months. It modulates *preferences* over *stale beliefs*. The lookahead arm confirms that the injection point rather than label quality is binding: `regime_oracle` sees the future and still yields +0.006 ($p = 0.88$).

## 7.2 Mechanism 2 — Regime-Conditioned Beliefs

Each historical day $d$ is weighted by

$$
w_d = \sum_{k} P(s_t = k \mid \mathcal{F}_t) \, P(s_d = k \mid \mathcal{F}_d)
\tag{9}
$$

the posterior probability that it shares today's latent state, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is the corresponding weighted second moment, shrunk toward a flat target with intensity $N / (N + n_{\mathrm{eff}})$ where $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective sample size. In a crisis, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is then built from past crisis days — higher volatility *and* higher cross-sectional correlation — so the optimizer sees the risk in its inputs rather than merely being told to fear it.

**Result: the effect is real but is not attributable to regimes.** `moments_hmm` gains +0.388 Sharpe at 150 bps, roughly 3.4× mechanism 1. But `moments_longwindow` — the same five-year lookback with **no regime weighting whatsoever** — gains +0.435. The entire benefit is the longer estimation window, which stabilizes $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, which stabilizes weights, which cuts turnover from 0.39 to 0.15.

This control arm was added only after the oracle probe showed a suspiciously large turnover reduction. Without it, this study would have reported a large and entirely spurious regime effect.

The objection to everything in Section 8.1 is reasonable, and we want to state it in its strongest form: *regime detection is supposed to change how you trade. # 9. Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## 9.1 Two Confounds the Controls Caught

Both of the following produced entirely plausible numbers and broke no test. We record them because each is a reusable warning, and because the second is the reason to trust the first.

**The falsification control had silently degenerated.** `ShuffledLabelDetector` originally sampled labels from the HMM's fitted transition matrix raised to the 21st power. The fitted chain is persistent for CALM (monthly self-transition ≈ 0.88) but not for the stressed states (≈ 0.28), so random draws almost never produced two consecutive turbulent or crisis months — and the minimum-dwell smoother, doing exactly its job of rejecting non-persistent labels, filtered them away. The control collapsed to 97% CALM with 1.1 switches per run, against the treatment's 83% and 10. It had become a static arm wearing a control's name, and because the CALM block is the low-turnover configuration, **it looked like the best arm in the study**. The fix was to permute the treatment arm's realized label episodes, preserving marginals and dwell times exactly while destroying only timing. Unit tests now assert both properties so it cannot silently regress.

**The mechanism-2 effect was almost entirely a window-length artifact.** The regime-weighted estimator needs a five-year lookback because weighting discards non-matching days, and that longer window is itself stabilizing. Adding `moments_longwindow` — flat covariance, same window, no regime model — showed it accounts for the whole effect and then some.

The general lesson: **when a mechanism changes more than one thing, every changed thing needs its own control arm.** Both confounds were invisible in the performance numbers and obvious the moment the right control existed.

## 9.2 Interpretation

Our answer to RQ3 is negative at both injection points, and our answer to RQ4 explains why the negative result is more informative than a marginal positive would have been. Regime conditioning in this setting is a mechanism for trading less. That is a real economic benefit — turnover reduction is worth money — but it is available directly, more cheaply, and more controllably through a turnover penalty, without fitting a hidden Markov model, without the estimation risk that comes with it, and without the implicit claim that the strategy knows something about the market's state.

The practical recommendation follows: **if a regime-conditioned strategy is reported to beat an unconditional baseline net of costs, the comparison that matters is against a turnover-matched control.** We are not aware of published regime-switching portfolio results that report one. Our own uncontrolled numbers (+0.113 and +0.388 Sharpe) are of the same magnitude as effects reported in that literature, and both are fully accounted for by turnover.

## 9.3 Limitations

The claim is about this setting, and the setting is specific. One universe (the 50 largest US common stocks), one objective, one metaheuristic, one evaluation window (2011–2024), and one asset class. The window contains few crisis episodes — roughly seven in fourteen years — so the mechanism is exercised rarely, and a study spanning 2000–2010 would give the regime mechanisms considerably more opportunity to matter. Our result does not establish that regime conditioning cannot work; it establishes that in this setting, at two natural injection points, the measured effect was turnover, and that a control capable of detecting this was necessary to find out.

We also note that the negative result is strongest exactly where the design is strongest. The oracle arms bound the mechanism's ceiling with perfect labels, and the turnover-matched controls close the confounding channel — but both are properties of *these* injection points. A mechanism that conditioned the expected-return vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, rather than $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ or the preference parameters, is untested here.

# 10. Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

We introduced FPSO, a hybrid of the Firefly Algorithm and Particle Swarm Optimization for cardinality- and bound-constrained portfolio selection, combining FA's diversity-preserving weighted-centroid attraction with PSO's personal- and global-best refinement under a repair-dominant constraint handler. Gross of transaction costs, FPSO is the strongest arm in our study.

We then used FPSO as the vehicle for a controlled evaluation of regime conditioning, injecting the regime signal into the optimizer's preferences and into its beliefs. Both mechanisms appear to work when measured the way the literature measures them, and neither survives control. A signal-free permutation control beats the treatment; a parameterization with no timing at all beats the timed one; perfect future knowledge of the regime buys nothing at either injection point; and a static arm calibrated to trade exactly as much as the regime arm beats it at every transaction-cost level, removing 95% and 97% of the cost-conditionality that made the mechanism look valuable.

**Regime effects in constrained portfolio optimization, in this setting, are turnover effects.** We release the harness that demonstrates it — the causal backtest with a machine-checked lookahead proof, matched-seed pairing, marginal- and dwell-preserving permutation controls, lookahead oracle bounds, and the turnover-matched control solver — because it caught two silent confounds in this very study, and because the protocol generalizes well beyond the mechanism we used it to test.

Future work follows directly from the limitations. The most informative next step is a third injection point, conditioning $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ rather than $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ or the preference block; the second is extending the evaluation window backward through 2000–2010 so the crisis mechanism is exercised more than seven times. On the optimizer side, FPSO would benefit from a study of the rebalancing period, which we fix at monthly without justification, and from seeding the swarm with previously high-performing fireflies to reduce search time. We also believe an FPSO-as-tuner architecture under a reinforcement-learning policy is worth investigating, though the finding of this paper is a caution: any such system must be evaluated against a turnover-matched control before its gains are attributed to what it learned.
