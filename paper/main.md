# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Modern Portfolio Theory, canonically formulated by @Markowitz1952,
frames portfolio construction as a mean-variance optimization problem
that seeks to minimize portfolio variance for a target level of expected
return. Despite the usefulness of this mean-variance (MV) framework,
practical investment settings often require additional constraints.
Investors may impose limits of the number of assets held (cardinality
constraints), minimum and maximum portfolio weights (bounding
constraints), transaction lot requirements, sector exposure limits, and
turnover budgets. These constraints can transform the original convex
optimization problem into a n mixed-integer optimization problem that is
much more complex in nature.

Classical exact solvers such as branch-and-bound and cutting-plane
methods scale poorly beyond a few hundred assets. Similarly,
gradient-based local search is fatally susceptible to the many local
optima generated by cardinality penalties. As a result,
biological-inspired metaheuristics---including Genetic Algorithms
(GA) [@Holland1975], Simulated Annealing (SA) [@Kirkpatrick1983], Tabu
Search (TS) [@Glover1989], and Particle Swarm Optimization
(PSO) [@Kennedy1995]---have been extensively applied to constrained
portfolio selection, offering population-based global search with no
gradient requirement while being able to explore large search spaces.

However, each method carries well-documented deficiencies: GA suffers
from premature convergence and parameter sensitivity; SA is
computationally slow for large populations; TS requires problem-specific
neighborhood design; and PSO is prone to loss of swarm diversity and
subsequent entrapment in local optima [@Engelbrecht2007].

More recently, the portfolio optimization problem has expanded to
include artificial intelligence methods that depart from traditional
search heuristics. Among these, Decision-Focused Learning (DFL) has
emerged as a powerful deep learning architecture that deviates from the
standard two-stage \"predict-then-optimize\" pipelines. Instead, DFL
embeds the optimization layer directly into the neural network's
training step, mapping asset features straight to portfolio weights. At
the same time, Large Language Models (LLMs) are being explored as
zero-shot portfolio allocators. Instead of relying purely on numerical
models, LLM optimization feeds historical prices, macro signals, and
fundamental metrics into generative models to build portfolios. However,
while these AI approaches offer strong predictive advantages, explicitly
managing complex, non-convex constraints remains a core challenge. This
is precisely the area where constraint-aware metaheuristics retain an
advantage. They are able to handle hard combinatorial constraints
natively, without requiring a gradient.

The Firefly Algorithm (FA), introduced by @Yang2008 [@Yang2009], is a
population-based optimization method in which each firefly represents a
candidate solution. The quality of a solution is measured by its
brightness, with better solutions assigned higher brightness values.
During the search process, fireflies move toward brighter fireflies, and
the strength of attraction decreases with distance. This mechanism
inherently partitions the swarm into local attraction basins and helps
maintain population diversity. However, while FA is effective at
exploring new solutions, it can have less precision near the global
optimum. This complementary weakness is precisely the inverse of PSO's
documented limitation.

The identification of complementary weaknesses between PSO and FA
provides clear motivation for hybridization: PSO offers refinement
capability but lacks diversity; FA offers diversity but lacks precision.
Recent successful hybrids combining PSO with genetic algorithms \[add
source here\] and collaborative neurodynamic optimization frameworks
incorporating PSO\[add source here\] have demonstrated that strategic
integration of complementary metaheuristics produces superior
performance in constrained portfolio problems, with improvements in
Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown metrics. A structured balance between
exploration and exploitation has been shown to be essential for solving
NP-hard portfolio optimization with cardinality constraint \[add source
here\]. Despite these successes, however the balance of exploration and
exploitation is still unresolved for a specific region: the cardinality
and bounding constraints generate a multimodal landscape where PSO loses
diversity too quickly to find optimal solutions, while FA spreads out
too much to achieve high precision. Neither method solves both issues on
its own.

We propose FPSO (Firefly-Particle Swarm Optimization), a hybrid
optimization algorithm that combines the diversity-maintaining strengths
of FA with the exploitation and refinement strengths of PSO, and (to our
knowledge) the first application of PSO-FA hybridization to
cardinality-constrainted portfolio optimization. While firefly-PSO
hybrids have appeared in other domains \[add source\], their use for
constrained portfolio selection, coupled with constraint-aware handling,
has not been explored. The algorithm will operate in two phases: FA
iterations drive exploration and maintain swarm diversity by leveraging
distance-based attraction across the full population, identifying
promising feasible regions; PSO iterations then refine solutions within
those regions through velocity-based updates, converging to local optima
with high precision. We adopt a phased rather than
continuously-integrated design deliberately: running FA and PSO update
rules simultaneously risks the two dynamics interfering - FA's
randomization disrupts PSO's convergence toward pbest/gbest, while PSO's
gbest attraction collapses the very diversity FA is meant to preserve.
This phasing separates the two regimes so each operates where it is most
effective. The phase transition is governed by a diversity metric that
detects when swarm dispersion has fallen below a threshold. This
directly addresses the core limitation of uneven balance of exploration
and exploitation that is seen in single-method approaches. The design is
validated by theoretical and empirical results: hybrid metaheuristics
have consistently outperformed standalone algorithms on benchmark
portfolio problems \[source\], and controlled studies show that
structred exploration-exploitation tradeoffs improve convergence in
mixed-integer optimization \[source\]. Preliminary experiments confirm
that FPSO outperforms both standalone PSO and FA on \[the name of our
dataset\], achieving \[inset metrics here\], full results are reported
in Section X.

For finance applications, we formulate FPSO within a multi-objective
framework that seeks to maximize absolute return---the raw annualized
return above a risk-free threshold.We favor the absolute return over the
Sharpe ratio as the primary objective for two reasons: the Sharpe ratio
penalizes upside volatility and assumes normally distributed returns,
both of which misrepresent the economic utility of active fund managers,
whose compensation is tied to realized absolute performance rather than
risk-adjusted ratios. Risk is retained as a secondary objective within
the multi-objective formulation, preserving the mean-variance logic of
the Markowitz setup while aligning the optimizer's primary target with
the economic utility it is meant to sever.

The primary contributions of this paper are:

- The FPSO algorithm: a formal hybridization of FA and PSO with a
  unified update rule and convergence analysis.

- A multi-objective absolute-return portfolio model incorporating
  cardinality, bounding, turnover, and sector constraints.

- Comprehensive back-test evidence across three major equity universes
  demonstrating FPSO's superiority over GA, SA, PSO, FA, and a passive
  benchmark.

- An ablation study isolating the contribution of each algorithmic
  component.

# Related Works {#sec:related}

Our work sits at the intersection of three research streams:
metaheuristic approaches to constrained portfolio selection, the Firefly
Algorithm and its hybridizations, and the recent wave of learning-based
portfolio construction. We review each in turn and identify the gap
FPSO addresses.

## Metaheuristics for Constrained Portfolio Selection

Augmenting the mean-variance model of @Markowitz1952 with cardinality
and bounding constraints transforms it into a mixed-integer quadratic
program for which exact solution scales poorly. @Chang2000 provided the
seminal metaheuristic treatment, adapting GA, TS, and SA to trace the
cardinality-constrained efficient frontier and establishing the public
benchmark instances used by much of the subsequent literature.
@WoodsideOriakhi2011 revisited these instances with improved population
heuristics, demonstrating that combining complementary search operators
yields measurably better frontiers than any single heuristic alone. A
broad survey by @Ertenlice2018 documents that swarm intelligence has
since become the dominant metaheuristic family for portfolio
optimization, with PSO the most frequently applied algorithm.

Within the PSO line, @Cura2009 first applied standalone PSO to the
cardinality-constrained problem, finding it competitive with GA, TS, and
SA and strongest on low-risk portfolios. @Zhu2011 reported that PSO
outperformed GA on Sharpe ratio across both constrained and
unconstrained formulations. However, @Deng2012 showed that standard PSO
stagnates on larger cardinality-constrained instances as swarm
diversity collapses, and proposed reflection-based modifications to
compensate. This premature-convergence failure mode is well documented
in the broader PSO literature [@Engelbrecht2007] and is the specific
weakness our hybridization targets.

## The Firefly Algorithm and Hybrid Metaheuristics

The Firefly Algorithm [@Yang2008; @Yang2009] replaces attraction to a
single global best with pairwise attraction that decays exponentially
with distance, causing the swarm to self-organize into multiple local
attraction basins. This makes FA naturally suited to the multimodal
landscapes that cardinality penalties induce, and a substantial
literature of FA variants and applications has developed
[@Fister2013]. In finance, @Bacanin2014 applied FA to
cardinality-constrained mean-variance portfolio selection with an
entropy diversity constraint, reporting results superior to GA, TS, SA,
and PSO baselines on standard benchmarks. The complementary weakness of
FA---slow refinement near optima due to persistent stochastic
perturbation---is equally well documented [@Fister2013].

Hybrids that pair FA's exploration with PSO's exploitation have shown
promise outside of finance: @Aydilek2018 proposed HFPSO, which
interleaves firefly-style local search within a PSO loop and
outperformed both standalone algorithms on computationally expensive
numerical benchmarks. Separately, weighted-centroid aggregation rules,
in which an agent moves toward an attractiveness-weighted centroid of
superior peers rather than a single leader, have proven effective in
other swarm-based estimation settings such as wireless sensor network
localization [@Yu2025]; FPSO's multi-peer centroid update
(Section [\[sec:algorithm\]](#sec:algorithm)) adapts this mechanism to
portfolio search. In
the portfolio domain itself, the closest work is the collaborative
neurodynamic framework of @Leung2022, which couples multiple projection
neural networks through a PSO-style information exchange to solve
cardinality-constrained portfolio selection. That approach, however,
relies on solving convex subproblems with gradient information at each
step, whereas FPSO is fully derivative-free and handles constraints
through a penalty-plus-repair scheme, making it agnostic to the
smoothness of the objective.

## Learning-Based Portfolio Construction

A parallel literature replaces search with learning. Decision-Focused
Learning trains the predictive model through the downstream
optimization layer so that forecast error is measured in decision
quality rather than statistical accuracy [@Elmachtoub2022]. Recent
ICAIF work has applied DFL to portfolio construction directly:
@Kim2025 learn covariance estimates end-to-end for the global minimum
variance portfolio, and @Lee2025 characterize how DFL reshapes return
forecasting models for mean-variance selection. In a different
direction, @Hou2024 evaluate LLMs as zero-shot allocators
that map prices, macro signals, and fundamentals to weights via
structured prompts. While these approaches offer strong predictive
power, embedding hard combinatorial constraints---cardinality, lots,
turnover budgets---into differentiable layers or generative prompts
remains largely unresolved, and simple heuristics remain difficult to
beat: @DeMiguel2009 famously showed that naive $1/N$ diversification
survives comparison with far more sophisticated estimators. We
therefore retain MVO, DFL, LLMO, and equal-weight allocations as
benchmarks in our empirical design
(Section [\[sec:benchmarks\]](#sec:benchmarks)).

## Positioning of This Work

In summary, metaheuristics handle hard portfolio constraints natively
but each standalone algorithm carries a documented exploration or
exploitation deficiency; FA--PSO hybrids that remedy exactly this
tradeoff exist but have not been applied to portfolio selection; and
learning-based allocators sidestep search entirely at the cost of
constraint fidelity. FPSO fills this gap: to our knowledge it is the
first FA--PSO hybrid for cardinality- and bound-constrained portfolio
optimization, combining FA's diversity-preserving centroid attraction
with PSO's personal- and global-best refinement under a
constraint-aware repair operator, and evaluated against classical,
naive, learning-based, and oracle benchmarks alike.

# Benchmarks {#sec:benchmarks}

## Classical

To rigorously evaluate the relative performance and mechanics of FPSO,
we implement several methods against FPSO:

1.  **Minimum Variance Optimisation (MVO):** Representing classical
    quantitative portfolio theory [@Markowitz1952], this baseline solves
    for the global minimum variance portfolio using empirical covariance
    matrices under identical boundary and leverage conditions,
    intentionally ignoring expected return signals to minimise
    structural downside variance.

2.  **Large Language Model Optimisation (LLMO):** Following contemporary
    explorations of zero-shot semantic portfolio allocators [@Hou2024],
    an advanced generative model (e.g., GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet) is
    supplied with historical price trajectories, macro signals, and
    fundamental metrics via structured prompts to generate weights,
    testing whether text-based market priors outperform systematic swarm
    dynamics.

3.  **Simulated Optimality (Oracle) Benchmark (SOB):** An unachievable
    look-ahead benchmark generated by running an exact mathematical
    solver (e.g., Gurobi) with perfect foresight of the next period's
    realized returns. This defines the empirical supremum of absolute
    returns within our constraint boundary.

4.  **Naive Equal Weight Benchmark (NEWB):** An equal-weighted portfolio
    mapping asset weights uniformly. This represents a highly robust
    model that eliminates estimation error and serves as the primary
    practical standard for active strategy validation [@DeMiguel2009].

5.  **Decision-Focused Learning (DFL):** Representing new deep learning
    architectures [@Kim2025; @Lee2025], this framework rejects standard
    two-stage systems that predict and then optimize. It leverages a
    neural network task loss that embeds the mean-variance optimization
    layer into the gradient backpropagation step, mapping asset features
    directly to parameter choices.

# Data Setup {#sec:setup}

The empirical data for this study is drawn from the Wharton Research
Data Services Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) daily
securities database. We observe daily equity metrics for all corporate
tickers comprising the S&P 500 index universe, mapped comprehensively
using their (`PERMNO`) numbers from January 1, 2010, through December
31, 2024. For each asset, the dataset includes historical daily closing
transaction prices, daily trading volumes to serve as our proxy for
liquidity and rolling returns. We normalize via Z-score normalization
for our data to avoid percentage skew.

# FPSO Algorithm {#sec:algorithm}

## Mathematical basis

The basis of any portfolio optimization problem is the decision of which
linear combinations of stocks and weights $w_i$ maximize the portfolio
return $R(w)$ where $\Sigma_i w_i =1$. We can express this relation as
$$\begin{equation}
    R(w)= \mu^T w - \lambda_v \sqrt{w^T \Sigma w} - \lambda_t \cdot \text{TO}(w, w_{\text{prev}})
\end{equation}$$ where $\mu^T w$ is the expected return (calculated from
given $\Sigma$, $\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}$ is the standard deviation of
volatility, $\text{TO}(w_t, w_{t-1})$ is the turnover (absolute
difference of the changes in our weights) and $\lambda_t$ is the
turnover weight.\
Our other constraints can come in the form of cardinality (number of
assets with $w_i \geq 0$ is bounded by $K$), capital
($\Sigma_i w_i \cdot p_i \leq V$ where $s_i$ is the price of each stock)
and lot constraints ($max(w_i) \leq u$), among others.

First, let's describe the equations that simulate the mechanics of our
system. Our fitness function is $$\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{w}) = R(\mathbf{w}) - \rho_1 \left| \sum_{i=1}^N w_i - 1 \right|- 
    \rho_2 \sum_{i=1}^N \left[(l_i - w_i)^2 + (0, w_i - u_i)^2 \right] - \rho_3 (\mathrm{card}(\mathbf{w}) - K)^2
\end{equation}$$

As we see $\rho_1$ is our weight restriction penalty, $\rho_2$ is our
lot penalty and $\rho_3$ is our cardinality penalty.

Brightness (or luminance) is defined for each particle $i$ at time $t$
as $$\begin{equation}
    I_i(t)=\Phi (x_i(t))-\Phi_{min(t)} +\epsilon
\end{equation}$$ Here, $\Phi$ is the worst fitness in the swarm and
epsilon is a small random (positive) constant.\
Once we find the brightness for all particles, we must calculate how the
particles move towards their brighter peers. In traditional PSO all
particles move towards the brightest particle in the swarm, but in FPSO
particles move towards the weighted centroid of all particles. We first
find the brighter peers $B_i(t)$ $$\begin{equation}
    B_i(t)= \{j:I_j(t) \gt I_i(t)\}
\end{equation}$$

Now, the centroid becomes $$\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}i(t) = \frac{\sum{j \in \mathcal{B_i}(t)} \beta_0 e^{-\gamma r{ij}^2} I_j(t) , \mathbf{x}j^{(t)}}{\sum{j \in \mathcal{B_i}(t)} \beta_0 e^{-\gamma r{ij}^2} I_j(t) + \delta}
\end{equation}$$ where we have $a_i (t)$ is the position based on the
centroid, $x_i(t)$ is the position of firefly i at time t. $x_j$ is the
position of a brighter firefly from $B_i(t)$ and $r_{i,j}$ =
$\Vert x_i(t) - x_j(t) \Vert_2$ is the Euclidean distance between them.
$\beta_0$ is the attractiveness at zero distance ($x_i = x_j$), $\gamma$
is the light absorption coefficient (dampening attraction based on
distance). $\delta$ is a small constant (preventing errors if $B_i(t)$
is the null set.

Our position updates as particles are drawn towards the centroid and
velocity can be calculated from the magnitude of the change.
$$\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i{(t+1)} = \mathbf{x}i{(t)} + \beta_0 e^{-\gamma r{ij}^2} (\mathbf{x}_j (t) - \mathbf{x}_i(t)) + \alpha \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_i(t)
\end{equation}$$ $a$ is the random coefficient and $\epsilon_i(t)$ is a
random uniform constant.

## FPSO Selection

From our CRSP securities universe we attempt to efficiently search the
portfolio space through FPSO by taking randomly disjoint permutations of
uniformly chosen random weights as baseline seeds for our algorithm. We
choose the highest performing seed and iterate onward from there. This
allows for a systematic 'guess' to be formed before refining our weights
and securities chosen through our algorithm.\
Not only is this relevant because of the search time reduction FPSO
grants, but because FPSO acts not only as a portfolio optimizer but an
effective stock selector as well. Results for this backtest can be found
in our results table below.\

## FPSO Oracle

Apart from testing FPSO's stock selection capacity, we also investigate
the actual portfolio optimization capacity of the algorithm
individually. We selected a benchmark portfolio (in this case: \[AAPL,
AMZN, JPM, JNJ, XOM, WMT, CAT, NEE, LIN, AMT\]). We selected these
stocks with two criteria in mind. First, ensuring our portfolio was a
diverse collection to limit biasing correlation. Second, excluding
stocks that grew extremely from 2010-2024 from a small baseline. Our
model does not look ahead for potential growth stocks, so this would not
have been an appropriate test.\
Backtesting this portfolio using FPSO yielded the results in our
performance table. To calculate the proportion of total gains captured,
we then implemented an oracle strategy. This would greedily assess the
maximum profit to be made by finding the maximum increase every
timestep. We then divide the FPSO-portfolio result by this (estimated)
optimum to find the proportion-of-optimality constant. Its value of 0.52
estimates how much of the total profits we were able to capture.\

# Conclusion

From our tests, we are able to conclude that Firefly Particle Swarm
Optimization is a statistically profitably strategy in both its general
search and specific portfolio analogs. This metaheuristic can serve as a
benchmark for future portfolio optimization studies and simultaneously
closes the gap of a lack of robust non-convex options for searching the
portfolio space.\
FPSO also compares well to its counterparts and captures a reasonably
high proportion of the optimal return for a specific portfolio. Futher
analysis is needed to see if this oracle test can be generalized for the
whole algorithm.\
FPSO could also benefit from investigating our rebalancing period
length. We currently rebalance each month (3̃0 timesteps) but this may
not be optimal. Additionally, FPSO's speed could also be improved beyond
its current levels (by sampling previous top performing fireflies as a
seed).\
Future directions for our work would work on a sensitivity analysis for
our FPSO results, an extended oracle work and a Reinforcement Learning
(RL) integration scheme. We believe using FPSO as a tuning mechanism
while letting an RL model set the general policy could alleviate the
issues of momentum tracking, fundamental analysis and regime change that
trouble the algorithm now.\
